How to Get a Business Address in Florida Without Renting Office Space

Published April 3, 2026 · 8 min read

Starting or running a business in Florida means you need a physical address. The state requires it for LLC registration. Banks require it to open a business account. Clients and vendors expect it on your invoices. But renting office space just for an address is expensive and, for most small businesses, unnecessary.

This guide covers every option available to Florida business owners who need a professional business address without the overhead of a traditional office lease. We will break down the costs, legal requirements, pros and cons of each approach, and help you decide which one fits your business.

Why You Need a Business Address in Florida

Before looking at options, it helps to understand why a business address matters in the first place. There are three main reasons:

LLC and Corporation Registration

When you file Articles of Organization for an LLC or Articles of Incorporation for a corporation with the Florida Division of Corporations (Sunbiz.org), you must provide a principal address and a mailing address. The principal address must be a physical street address in Florida. P.O. Boxes are not accepted for the principal address field. If you operate your business from home, you can technically use your home address, but that comes with significant downsides covered below.

Professional Credibility

Your business address appears on your website, business cards, invoices, contracts, Google Business Profile, and all correspondence. A professional commercial address signals stability and legitimacy to clients, vendors, and partners. For businesses that serve local clients in South Florida, having a recognizable Broward County or Miami-Dade address builds trust immediately.

Mail and Package Receiving

Businesses receive mail. Tax notices from the IRS and Florida Department of Revenue, legal documents, bank statements, vendor invoices, client checks, and packages all need somewhere to go. If you work remotely, travel frequently, or run your business from multiple locations, having a dedicated address with professional mail handling solves a real logistical problem.

Your Options: Four Ways to Get a Business Address

There are four main options for Florida business owners who need an address without a full office lease. Each has trade-offs.

Option 1: Use Your Home Address

The simplest option is using your residential address. It is free, and it is legal for most business types in Florida. However, there are real problems:

  • Privacy: Your home address becomes a public record. Anyone who searches your LLC on Sunbiz.org will see where you live. This is a significant concern for business owners who value personal privacy or who operate in industries where disputes are common.
  • Credibility: A residential address on business materials can undermine the professional image you are trying to build. Clients and partners may perceive your business as less established.
  • HOA and zoning: Some homeowner associations and local zoning ordinances restrict commercial activity at residential addresses. Receiving frequent business mail or packages could create issues.
  • Mail mixing: Personal and business mail arrive at the same place. This creates accounting headaches and makes it harder to maintain clean records.

Bottom line: Free, but you sacrifice privacy, credibility, and separation between personal and business life.

Option 2: Rent a P.O. Box

A USPS P.O. Box costs $20 to $60 per month depending on size and location. It gives you a dedicated mailing address separate from your home. But there are hard limitations:

  • Not accepted for LLC registration: The Florida Division of Corporations requires a physical street address for the principal address of an LLC or corporation. A P.O. Box does not qualify.
  • Not accepted by all banks: Many banks will not open a business account with only a P.O. Box address.
  • No package receiving: USPS P.O. Boxes do not accept packages from UPS, FedEx, or DHL. If your business receives packages from non-USPS carriers, a P.O. Box will not work.
  • No professional image: A P.O. Box address tells clients you do not have a physical office. Some customers interpret this as a sign of a less established business.

Bottom line: Cheap, but too limited for most business needs. Cannot be used as your LLC principal address.

Option 3: Virtual Office (Best Option for Most Businesses)

A virtual office gives you a real commercial street address with a unique suite number. You can use it for LLC registration, banking, licensing, marketing, and all business correspondence. The best virtual office providers also include mail handling, package receiving, conference room access, and additional services like notary and registered agent.

  • Real street address: Accepted everywhere a physical address is required, including Sunbiz.org, banks, Google Business Profile, and professional licensing boards like FREC.
  • Privacy: Your home address stays private. Only your business address appears on public records.
  • Professional mail handling: Mail is received, sorted, scanned, and forwarded. You get notifications when mail arrives. No trips to a P.O. Box.
  • Packages from all carriers: USPS, UPS, FedEx, DHL, and Amazon are all accepted.
  • Low cost: Depending on the provider, a virtual office ranges from $39 per month to $449 per month. Local providers tend to be significantly cheaper than national chains.
  • Additional services: Many providers offer conference room access, notary services, registered agent service, and phone answering.

Bottom line: The best combination of cost, credibility, privacy, and functionality for most small businesses.

Option 4: Coworking Space

Coworking spaces like WeWork, Regus, or local operators provide shared office environments. Some offer a dedicated business address as part of a membership. Prices typically start at $150 to $300 per month for a basic "virtual" membership that includes an address and limited mail handling, and go up to $500+ per month for a dedicated desk.

  • Address included: Most coworking memberships include a business address.
  • Workspace access: You can use shared desks, meeting rooms, and common areas.
  • Higher cost: Significantly more expensive than a virtual office if you only need an address.
  • Contracts: Many coworking spaces require 3 to 12 month commitments.
  • Turnover risk: Coworking spaces frequently close, merge, or relocate. If your provider shuts down, your business address changes, which means updating your LLC, bank accounts, licenses, and all business materials.

Bottom line: Good if you actually need a physical workspace. Expensive and risky if you only need an address.

What is a CMRA — and Is It the Only Model?

CMRA stands for Commercial Mail Receiving Agency. It is a USPS designation for a third-party business whose service is receiving mail on behalf of other people or companies. It is one of the legal frameworks under which business address services operate — but not the only one.

When you sign up for a virtual office at a CMRA, you complete USPS Form 1583. This is a federal form that authorizes the CMRA to receive mail on your behalf. You must provide two forms of identification and have the form notarized. Once on file, your mail is legally delivered to your suite number at the CMRA address.

A different lawful model exists when the building owner licenses business presence directly: you hold a license to use a suite designation at the owner's own commercial property, and any mail that arrives is received incidentally under that license — not as a third-party mail-receiving service. Because no third-party mail agency is involved, USPS Form 1583 does not apply. Wilton Plaza operates this way: Wilton Plaza is not a CMRA — the building owner (since 1973) licenses business presence directly under a written service agreement, so no Form 1583 is involved. Whichever model a provider operates under, judge them on transparency: who owns the building, what the written agreement actually covers, and whether the location is real and staffed during business hours.

How a Virtual Office Works in Practice

The process is straightforward:

  1. Sign up and choose a plan. Select the service level that matches your needs: address only, address with mail scanning, or full virtual office with all services included.
  2. Complete the provider's onboarding paperwork. At a CMRA, this means USPS Form 1583: provide identification and have the form notarized (many providers have a notary on staff). At an owner-licensed provider like Wilton Plaza, you sign a written service agreement instead — no Form 1583 is involved.
  3. Receive your suite number. You get a unique suite number at the provider's commercial address. This is your business address going forward.
  4. Update your registrations. Use your new address on Sunbiz.org, bank accounts, business licenses, Google Business Profile, website, and marketing materials.
  5. Mail arrives and is handled. The provider receives your mail, scans it or photographs it, and emails you a notification. You can request forwarding, pickup, or shredding.

Day to day, you receive email notifications whenever mail arrives. You view scanned mail from anywhere. If something needs your physical signature or in-person attention, you visit the office. Otherwise, everything is handled remotely.

What to Look for When Choosing a Virtual Office

Not all virtual offices are equal. Here are the factors that matter most:

Does the Provider Own the Building?

This is the single most important factor most people overlook. If your virtual office provider leases their space, they can be forced out by the landlord. If the building is sold, the new owner may not renew the lease. If the provider goes out of business, the lease ends. In any of these scenarios, your business address disappears and you have to update every registration, bank account, license, and piece of marketing material.

A provider that owns the building eliminates this risk entirely. Your address is as permanent as the building itself.

Is There an On-Site Notary?

If your provider is a CMRA, you need a notary for Form 1583 during onboarding. Either way, you will need notary services throughout the life of your business for contracts, affidavits, and other legal paperwork. Having a licensed notary on staff at the same location as your business address saves time and trips across town.

What Does It Cost?

Virtual office pricing varies enormously. National chains like Regus and Davinci charge $79 to $449 per month. Local providers can offer the same services for $39 to $79 per month. Setup fees, deposits, and long-term contracts are common at larger providers. Look for transparent pricing with no hidden costs.

Are There Contracts or Commitments?

What matters most is not the length of the commitment but whether the terms are transparent. Look for clear pricing, quarterly or annual billing, commitments stated up front, and no hidden fees or surprise charges at renewal. Read the written agreement before you sign, whatever the provider.

What Services Are Included?

Compare what is included in the base price versus what costs extra. Key questions:

  • How many mail scans per month?
  • Is package receiving included for all carriers?
  • Is conference room access included?
  • Is registered agent service available?
  • Is notary included or extra?
  • Are there mail forwarding fees?
