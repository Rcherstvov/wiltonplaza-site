Florida Virtual Office: What It Is, What It Costs, and What It Cannot Do

Published September 18, 2026 · By Wilton Plaza

A Florida virtual office gives your business a real commercial street address, a place that receives your mail, and amenities you use by the hour, without renting an office. In 2026 it costs from about $30 to $150 a month depending on what is bundled. What it cannot do is act as your registered agent by default, guarantee that a bank will accept it, or receive certified mail and legal process on your behalf.

This guide is written for people comparing providers across Florida, not only in one city. It covers what the term means, what is actually inside a plan, what the market charges, and the four limits every honest provider will tell you about before you pay. If you already know you want a Broward County address, the Virtual Office plan and Business Address plan pages have the specifics.

What "Virtual Office" Means in Florida

The phrase is used loosely online, so it helps to separate three things that are often sold under one name.

  • A business address. A street address at a real building that you can put on your Florida LLC filing, your website, your invoices and your bank paperwork. This is the core of every plan.
  • Mail receiving. Someone at that address accepts your letters and packages, logs them, and holds them for pickup or forwards them on request. In Florida this is regulated by the USPS: the provider must be a registered Commercial Mail Receiving Agency (CMRA), and you sign USPS Form 1583 to authorize it.
  • On-site amenities. A conference room you can reserve, a notary on site, a front desk that answers questions during business hours. Plans called "virtual office" usually include some hours of these; plans called "business address" usually do not.

Two things are not part of a virtual office, even though providers advertise them nearby: registered agent service, which is a separate legal role under Florida Statute 605.0113 and is engaged separately, and a private desk or office, which is coworking or a lease.

What You Can Do With the Address

Once your service is activated and you have the approved address format, the address works for the everyday paperwork of running a company.

  • Florida LLC filing. Principal office address and mailing address on Sunbiz, at formation or by amendment.
  • IRS and EIN. The mailing address on Form SS-4 and later IRS correspondence. See using a business address for an EIN.
  • Bank, Stripe and marketplaces. The business address you enter when you open accounts. Each institution applies its own rules, which is the first limit below.
  • Your website, Google Business Profile and invoices. A commercial address instead of your home, which is the reason most owners start looking. See keeping your home address off public records.
  • Mail. Letters and packages are received during business hours, logged, and held for pickup. Forwarding and scanning are available on request; the plan page lists what is included and what is charged.

Four Things a Florida Virtual Office Cannot Do

These are the points where providers are tempted to over-promise. Knowing them before you pay saves the most common disappointments.

1. It cannot guarantee a bank will accept it

Banks decide under their own account-opening rules. A real commercial address at an owner-operated building is commonly accepted, and a real building with a staffed front desk helps when a bank verifies it. But some banks require an in-person branch visit, and digital banks apply their own screening. No provider can promise acceptance, and a provider who does is telling you something they do not control.

2. It is not your registered agent

Every Florida LLC needs a registered agent with a Florida street address who is available during business hours to accept legal papers. That is a separate role with separate obligations. A virtual office address on its own is not a registered agent address, and using it as one without an agreement is a breach of most providers' terms. Read do I need a registered agent in Florida before you file.

3. It does not sign for certified mail or legal process

Certified, registered and restricted-delivery mail, court papers and time-sensitive government notices are not signed for at a CMRA on your behalf. If you expect that kind of mail, keep a separate monitored address for it. This is the honest reason the "we handle everything" pitch should make you cautious.

4. It is not a place to run inventory or fulfillment

Virtual office plans are for business mail. Pallets, e-commerce returns, drop-shipping and high-volume packages are refused or charged separately at every serious provider, because a CMRA is not a warehouse.

How to Choose a Provider in Florida

  1. Check that the address is a real commercial building and that the provider operates it. Search the address on Google Maps and look at the tenants. A converted mailbox store or a coworking sublease behaves differently when a bank calls.
  2. Ask for the CMRA registration. If the provider cannot say that USPS Form 1583 is part of onboarding, mail handling is informal.
  3. Read the mail rules. How long mail is held free, what forwarding costs, whether scanning is included, and what happens to mail after you cancel.
  4. Look for the setup fee and the term. Many national plans quote a low monthly price plus a setup fee and a month-to-month term that can be cancelled while the address is still on your filings. A 12-month term with no setup fee is the opposite trade: more commitment, lower total cost, and an address that stays stable on Sunbiz.
  5. Confirm who answers the phone. A staffed front desk during business hours matters when a courier, a bank verifier or a client shows up.

Which Florida City Should the Address Be In?

For a Florida LLC, Sunbiz does not care which city the principal address is in as long as it is a real street address. Banks and marketplaces care that it is commercial and verifiable. Clients care whether it looks credible on an invoice. A Broward County address between Fort Lauderdale and Miami does all three at a fraction of a Brickell tower price, which is why most owners outside the state pick a suburban commercial building rather than a downtown skyscraper. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a virtual office in Florida?

A virtual office is a licensed business presence at a real commercial address without renting a private office. In Florida it typically bundles a street address you can use for your LLC, mail receiving at a USPS-registered Commercial Mail Receiving Agency (CMRA), and on-site amenities such as a conference room or notary that you book when needed.

How much does a virtual office cost in Florida?

Address-only plans in South Florida run roughly $30 to $80 a month, and plans with a conference room and staffed reception run about $50 to $150. Wilton Plaza charges $39 a month for a Business Address plan and $49 a month for a Virtual Office plan, billed quarterly or annually, with no setup fee.

Can I use a Florida virtual office address for my LLC on Sunbiz?

A real commercial street address can be used as the principal office and mailing address of a Florida LLC once the provider has activated your service and given you the approved address format. It is not automatically your registered agent address; registered agent service is a separate engagement.

Will a bank accept a virtual office address?

Each bank decides under its own account-opening rules. A real commercial address at an owner-operated building is commonly accepted, but a virtual office provider cannot promise that any bank, payment processor or marketplace will accept it.

Do I have to visit Florida to set up a virtual office?

At Wilton Plaza, no. Onboarding is online: payment, a short form, a photo ID and a proof of address, then a short verification video call. USPS Form 1583 authorizes the CMRA to receive your mail.

The Bottom Line

A Florida virtual office is a real address, a mailroom and a few hours of amenities, priced from about $30 to $150 a month. Judge providers by the building, the CMRA registration, the mail rules and the term, and treat any promise about banks or registered agent service as a warning sign rather than a feature.
